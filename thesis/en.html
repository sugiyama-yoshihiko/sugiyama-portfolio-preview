Artistic Practice for Scene-Making- Beginning with Pottery-Making -

Master’s thesis · March 2026

Preface

This thesis discusses the concept of “scene-making” through my artistic practice, which has primarily involved making and exhibiting work in ceramics. In this thesis, a “scene” does not refer simply to a visual landscape or a spatial composition. Rather, it is a concept that encompasses the space arising as a whole from relationships between people, their surrounding environment, and the layering of acts of making and time.

As people gather, work with their hands, and make things while talking together, relationships and a working environment specific to that place emerge. This thesis understands such a dynamic, continually renewed space as a “scene,” and defines the artistic practice of intentionally bringing it into being as “scene-making.” Scene-making is a practice concerned not only with finished works, but also with the process through which relationships emerge and change through the act of making itself.

There is no prefecture in the Japanese archipelago where Jōmon pottery has not been excavated, and pottery-making was once an indispensable part of sustaining everyday life. Gathering earth, giving it form, and firing it were necessary skills shared within local communities, rather than activities confined to particular specialists or institutions.

In the present day, however, industrialization has greatly transformed the role of pottery as an everyday necessity. At the same time, ceramics as a field, supported by electric kilns, specialized facilities, and institutionalized educational settings, has developed a structure that tends to limit who can make work. Kilns in particular require space, funding, and the ability to manage them, and in this sense can function as devices that make the act of making itself a privilege.

Meanwhile, the widespread availability of pottery classes and hands-on pottery sessions demonstrates that pottery-making remains an appealing activity. Many of these, however, are designed as experiences in which participants consume predetermined processes and outcomes. In this respect, they differ from the pottery-making of the past, which was continuous with everyday life.

Taking as its starting point the situation in which pottery-making was once a necessity but is now privileged and commodified, this thesis examines the possibilities of scene-making as an artistic practice by reconsidering pottery-making as a medium for reconnecting people, places, and time.

Introduction

Drawing on the context of socially engaged art (hereafter SEA), this thesis considers how the generation of relationships and the qualities that constitute a work of art can coexist through ceramics, a form of making that produces material outcomes. It examines two practices: the Banana Pottery Club, which I organize, and the Katsurao-yaki Project, for which I am responsible for planning and technical design.

Both practices are characterized by their use of simple firing methods rather than reliance on permanent facilities such as electric kilns. In doing so, they put into perspective the privileges associated with facilities and institutions that are inherent in pottery-making. Pottery-making is thus reconfigured as a process that can be shared and renewed through relationships, rather than an activity confined to particular places or specialists.

This thesis pays particular attention to the ways in which participatory experiences can lead to long-term relationships and an accumulation of making, rather than remaining fleeting acts of consumption. Unlike pottery classes or hands-on workshops, these practices intentionally establish a structure through which the act of making itself continues to leave a presence in a place. In this respect, they also differ from conventional SEA.

In the practices examined in this study, I share with participants the same acts of pottery-making that I carry out in my everyday practice. The conditions of making, including its demands, uncertainties, and possibilities of failure, are made visible. A defining feature of this practice is that experiences are not consumed and exhausted, but continue to remain in the place as an accumulation of relationships and a working environment.

Chapter 1: The Banana Pottery Club

Section 1: What Is the Banana Pottery Club?

The Banana Pottery Club is an art project that I organize and run at the shared studio Banana※1 in Iwakura, Aichi Prefecture. As a rule, several people gather every Wednesday and carry out the entire pottery-making process, from shaping clay to firing.

Most participants have no specialist knowledge of ceramics and only limited experience of making. For this reason, the activity does not primarily aim to teach skills, but emphasizes the emergence of relationships through sharing the making process. The relationships formed here are neither those of master and apprentice nor those based on evaluation. They are relationships on an equal footing, grounded in sharing the same processes and time. As these relationships have accumulated over time, a working environment unique to the Banana Pottery Club has taken shape.

In July and November 2025, we participated in exhibitions to present the outcomes of our activities, developing a practice that moved back and forth between making and exhibiting.

Section 2: The Banana Pottery Club’s Methodology

The methodology of the Banana Pottery Club consists of the following steps.

  • Fire work using simple equipment such as charcoal starters and tin cans, rather than existing facilities such as electric kilns.
  • Carry out all stages, from making to firing, in the same space, making the process visible.
  • Continue making and firing during exhibitions, treating the acts themselves as part of the work.
  • Document conversations and collaborative activities through video, photographs, and writing, and reconfigure these records for exhibition.

These methods aim to detach pottery-making from particular institutions and facilities and to share it as an everyday act of making. Many workshop-based practices offer special experiences that differ from the artist’s own practice. What distinguishes this practice is that it shares the very pottery-making that I carry out in my everyday life.

Section 3: Reflections on and Summary of the Banana Pottery Club

The Banana Pottery Club has departed from the skill-centered structure of conventional pottery classes and has functioned as a place for sharing the process of making itself. In exhibitions, not only finished works but also traces of making and relationships among participants were made visible in the space and presented as a scene.

During making sessions, I frequently observed situations in which the forms and actions of someone working nearby influenced the direction of another person’s work. For example, seeing the thin rim of another person’s vessel prompted someone to remake their own work with a thinner rim. In other instances, referring to another person’s motifs led to new forms. These changes did not result from imitation or instruction, but arose spontaneously within the group.

This process, in which relationships with others change the outcome of making, is a concrete instance of the scene-making addressed in this thesis. Rather than being completed entirely within the individual, making is renewed through interactions with what lies outside the self. In this respect, scene-making has a temporality and spatiality distinct from individual practice. At the same time, this activity depends substantially on my involvement as its organizer, leaving the sustainability of its operation and the distribution of agency as issues for the future. Nevertheless, the practice has demonstrated to some extent that scenes arising through ongoing making can also be shared with viewers.

Chapter 2: The Katsurao-yaki Project

Section 1: What Is the Katsurao-yaki Project?

The Katsurao-yaki Project developed from work I made as a participant in the Hamakaru Art Project※2 in 2024. In this project, earth from Katsurao Village in Futaba District, Fukushima Prefecture, is used to shape and fire pottery, establishing “Katsurao-yaki” as a new form of local pottery. In Katsurao Village, where the transmission of culture and skills has faced difficulties since the earthquake disaster, the project seeks to foster culture through pottery-making by positioning villagers as those who will carry it forward and by establishing a charcoal kiln.

Section 2: The Katsurao-yaki Project’s Methodology

The Katsurao-yaki Project consists of the following steps.

  • Use earth and clay collected within Katsurao Village.
  • Fire work in a newly established charcoal kiln within the village.
  • Hold multiple workshops to share the making process and make it visible.
  • Produce and publish video and written documentation.

These practices aim not to preserve or exhibit culture, but to establish conditions under which it can be passed on. The project goes beyond making short-term relationships visible: it places importance on leaving the working environment itself within the community.

Section 3: Reflections on and Summary of the Katsurao-yaki Project

This project emphasizes the sustainability of the working environment rather than short-term outcomes. The installation of a charcoal kiln and the presence of people who will carry the practice forward establish the physical and social conditions for scene-making to take root in the community.

As part of the project’s future development, plans for production and distribution aimed at both businesses and individual consumers are currently taking shape. Specifically, these envisage production in collaboration with companies and organizations outside the region, sales to individual consumers, and distribution through e-commerce sites. These are not merely economic activities, but attempts to extend a structure in which making, use, and relationships circulate, both within and beyond the region.

The project’s ultimate goal is to establish a situation in which production and the local economy circulate autonomously within the community, without my intervention. Achieving this may entail a certain sense of loss for me, but accepting this transition in my role is itself an important element of the project.

Craft is said to undergo successive stages of change before it takes root in a region: project, program, movement, and culture. I see my role as extending as far as the formation of a movement, and believe that subsequent development should be entrusted to the community.

Conclusion

This thesis has examined the methods and significance of scene-making that begins with pottery-making through the practices of the Banana Pottery Club and the Katsurao-yaki Project. Both practices attempt to put into perspective the privileges associated with facilities and institutions that the field of ceramics has come to contain. They do so by distancing themselves from established conditions of production, such as electric kilns and specialized facilities, and adopting simple firing methods and structures for collaborative making. As a result, they have demonstrated that making need not be limited to particular skilled practitioners or places, but can be reconfigured as a shareable process.

The contribution of this research lies in presenting a framework in which the generation of relationships and the qualities that constitute a work of art can coexist through making that yields material outcomes, while connecting with the context of SEA. In particular, its emphasis on opening up the act of making itself and fostering ongoing relationships and working environments distinguishes it from conventional workshop-based practices.

At the same time, the effects of scene-making are difficult to make visible in the short term, and cannot readily be assessed by the presence or number of completed works. The need to examine these practices from a long-term perspective, including the conditions under which working environments are maintained and relationships continue to be renewed, remains an issue for future research.

Scene-making that begins with pottery-making is not confined to a particular region or project, but is a practice that can be applied in any place or context. The methods and perspectives presented in this thesis constitute an exploratory proposal for reconsidering ceramics as a more open field. Scene-making that begins with pottery-making is also a practice that reexamines how making, once a necessity of everyday life, has become privileged and commodified. Through simple firing methods and structures for collaborative making, this study has presented one attempt to reposition ceramics as an activity that can once again be shared.
